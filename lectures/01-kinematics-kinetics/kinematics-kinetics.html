Describing movements with numbers

Movement description beyond words

Movements are changes in the position of an object of interest over time.

Position is a vector quantity describing the spatial location of an object with respect to a chosen reference frame.

A reference frame is two independent choices: an origin \(O_{A}\) (where \(x=0\) sits) and a basis \(\v{x}_{A}\) (a direction and a length).

With respect to this reference frame \(A\), the positions of particles \(P_1\) and \(P_2\) are given as follows, \[ \v{r}_1^{A} = \m{3.2} \qquad \v{r}_2^{A} = \m{-1.5} \]

where \(\v{r}_{1}^{A}, \v{r}_2^{A} \in \mathbb{R}\). The superscript \(A\) says the position is expressed in reference frame \(A\); the subscripts \(1\) and \(2\) are the particle numbers. A subscript also labels the frame’s own parts, e.g. \(O_A\). The numbers are the coefficients of \(\v{r}\) in the basis of that frame, which here is just \(\v{x}_{A}\).

Who decides the reference frame?

Why did we choose one reference frame over another?

Anyone! We choose what is natural and convenient for the problem at hand.

Both the origin and the basis are fair game.

In this course, we will only choose an orthonormal basis — basis vectors have unit length and are mutually orthogonal.

Reference frame \(B\) is translated and rotated with respect to reference frame \(A\). Every point in the 1D space can be represented in either frame, but the numbers will be different.

Changing between reference frames

How do we change between reference frames?

Two pieces of information to change between reference frames:

  1. Rotation of the basis of the target (\(B\)) w.r.t. the base frame (\(A\)). Represented by a rotation matrix \(\v{R}_{B}^{A}\).

  2. Translation of the origin of the target frame (\(B\)) w.r.t. the base frame (\(A\)), represented in the base frame. Represented by a translation vector \(\v{o}_{B}^{A}\).

Changing between reference frames

Rotation Matrix

Rotation Matrix: \(\v{R}_{B}^{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 1}\) — Represents what the basis vectors of the frame \(B\) (subscript) look like in the frame \(A\) (superscript).

\[ \v{R}_{B}^{A} = \m{\v{x}_{A}^\top \v{x}_{B}} \]

In the above example, \(\v{R}_{B}^{A} = \m{-1}\), because \(\Vert \v{x}_A \Vert = 1\), \(\Vert \v{x}_B \Vert = 1\) and \(\theta = 180^\circ\).

Changing between reference frames

Translation Vector

Translation Vector: \(\v{o}_{B}^{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 1}\) — Represents the position of the origin of the frame \(B\) (subscript) in the frame \(A\) (superscript). In the above example, \(\v{o}_{B}^{A} = \m{6}\).

The full description of the reference frame \(B\) w.r.t. the reference frame \(A\) is given by the pair \(\left( \v{R}_{B}^{A}, \v{o}_{B}^{A} \right)\).

This pair can be used to transform any vector expressed in frame \(B\) to frame \(A\).

Changing between reference frames

Going back and forth between frames

Given the pair \(\left( \v{R}_{B}^{A}, \v{o}_{B}^{A} \right)\), we can transform any vector expressed in frame \(B\) to frame \(A\) using the following equation:

\[ \v{r}_{i}^{A} = \v{R}_{B}^{A} \, \v{r}_{i}^{B} + \v{o}_{B}^{A} \]

Product of a matrix with a vector results in a vector.

In 1D, this product is very simple. We simply multiply the individual elements of the matrix and the vector.

Changing between reference frames

Going back and forth between frames

Given the pair \(\left( \v{R}_{B}^{A}, \v{o}_{B}^{A} \right)\), how do we go the other way around? \(\v{r}_{i}^{A} \mapsto \v{r}_{i}^{B}\).

\[ \v{r}_{i}^{B} = {\v{R}_{B}^{A}}^{-1} \left( \v{r}_{i}^{A} - \v{o}_{B}^{A} \right) = \v{R}_{A}^{B} \v{r}_{i}^{A} + \v{o}_{A}^{B} \]

where, \({\v{R}_{B}^{A}}^{-1} = \v{R}_{A}^{B}\), and \(\v{o}_{A}^{B} = -{\v{R}_{B}^{A}}^{-1}\v{o}_{B}^{A}\).

We obtain \({\v{R}_{B}^{A}}^{-1}\) by inverting the single number in the matrix. So, for the above example, we have, \[ {\v{R}_{B}^{A}}^{-1} = \v{R}_{A}^{B} = \m{\frac{1}{-1}} = \m{-1} \qquad \v{o}_{A}^{B} = -{\v{R}_{B}^{A}}^{-1} \v{o}_{B}^{A} = -\m{-1}\m{6} = \m{6} \]

The linear algebra we need

Vectors, matrices and a few of their operations

Vectors \(\mathbb{R}^n\) are an ordered collection of real numbers \(\mathbb{R}\). We will also write them as a column and call them column vectors.

\[ \v{x} = \m{x_1 \\ x_2 \\ \vdots \\ x_n} \]

The transpose operation changes a column vector to a row vector and vice versa.

\[ \v{x}^\top = \m{x_1 & x_2 & \cdots & x_n} \qquad \qquad \left(\v{x}^\top\right)^\top = \v{x} \]

Addition and scaling rules and their geometric interpretation are the same as that in \(\mathbb{R}^2\).

2-Norm: Consider a vector \(\v{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n\). \(\Vert \v{x} \Vert = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2 }\)

Standard inner product: Consider two vectors, \(\v{x}, \v{y} \in \mathbb{R}^n\).

\[ \v{x}^\top\v{y} = \m{x_1 & x_2 & \cdots & x_n}\m{y_1 \\ y_2 \\ \vdots \\ y_n} = \sum_{i=1}^n x_iy_i = \Vert \v{x} \Vert \Vert \v{y} \Vert \cos \left(\theta\right)\]

where, \(\theta\) is the angle between \(\v{x}\) and \(\v{y}\).

The linear algebra we need

Vectors, matrices and a few of their operations

Matrices \(\mathbb{R}^{p \times q}\) are a rectangular arrangement of numbers \(\mathbb{R}\).

\[ \v{A} = \m{a_{11} & a_{12} & \cdots & a_{1q} \\ a_{21} & a_{22} & \cdots & a_{2q} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ a_{p1} & a_{p2} & \cdots & a_{pq}} \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times q}\]

where, \(a_{ij}\) is the component of the matrix in the \(i^{th}\) row and \(j^{th}\) column. When \(p = q\), \(\v{A}\) is a square matrix, else it’s a rectangular matrix.

With this definition, we can think of column vectors as a matrix with a single column, and a row vector as a matrix with a single row.

We can also think of the matrix \(\v{A}\) as a row of column vectors from \(\mathbb{R}^p\).

\[ \v{A} = \m{\v{a}_{1} & \v{a}_{2} & \cdots & \v{a}_{q}} \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times q}\]

where, \(\v{a}_{i} \in \mathbb{R}^p\) is the \(i^{th}\) column of \(\v{A}\).

Transpose of a matrix switches the rows and columns. \(\v{A}^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{q \times p}\).

\[ \v{A}^\top = \m{a_{11} & a_{21} & \cdots & a_{p1} \\ a_{12} & a_{22} & \cdots & a_{p2} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ a_{1q} & a_{2q} & \cdots & a_{pq}} \in \mathbb{R}^{q \times p}\]

Matrix-vector product Let \(\v{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times q}\) and \(\v{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{q \times 1}\). We can define this product as the following,

\[ \mathbb{R}^{p} \ni \v{y} = \v{A}\v{x} = \m{a_{11} & a_{12} & \cdots & a_{1q} \\ a_{21} & a_{22} & \cdots & a_{2q} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ a_{p1} & a_{p2} & \cdots & a_{pq}} \m{x_1 \\ x_2 \\ \vdots \\ x_q} = \m{\sum_{i=1}^qa_{1i} x_i \\ \sum_{i=1}^qa_{2i} x_i \\ \vdots \\ \sum_{i=1}^qa_{pi} x_i}\]

We can also view \(\v{A}\v{x}\) as the following,

\[ \v{y} = \v{A}\v{x} = \m{\v{a}_{1} & \v{a}_{2} & \cdots & \v{a}_{q}} \m{x_1 \\ x_2 \\ \vdots \\ x_q} = \sum_{i=1}^q x_i \v{a}_{i}\]

Matrix-Matrix product Let \(\v{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times q}\) and \(\v{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{q \times r}\). Then we can define the product of these two matrices as the following,

\[ \mathbb{R}^{p \times r} \ni \v{C} = \m{c_{11} & c_{12} & \cdots & c_{1r} \\ c_{21} & c_{22} & \cdots & c_{2r} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ c_{p1} & c_{p2} & \cdots & c_{pr}} = \v{A}\v{B} = \m{a_{11} & a_{12} & \cdots & a_{1q} \\ a_{21} & a_{22} & \cdots & a_{2q} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ a_{p1} & a_{p2} & \cdots & a_{pq}} \m{b_{11} & b_{12} & \cdots & b_{1r} \\ b_{21} & b_{22} & \cdots & b_{2r} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ b_{q1} & b_{q2} & \cdots & b_{qr}} \qquad c_{ij} = \sum_{k} a_{ik}b_{kj}\]

Matrix multiplication \(\v{A}\v{B}\) is allowed if and only if the number of columns of \(\v{A}\) equals the number of rows of \(\v{B}\).

Representing kinematics of rigid bodies

How many numbers do we need?

A rigid body is a collection of points whose relative spatial location with respect to each other does not change no matter the forces acting on these points.

Useful approximation for many applications, simplifying representing/analysing kinematics of real objects.

We need a total of 6 numbers to represent its position and orientation w.r.t. a reference frame.

\[ \text{Rigid body in 3D space} \longrightarrow \left( \alpha, \beta, \gamma, x, y, z\right)\]

\(\left(\alpha, \beta, \gamma\right)\) represent the orientation of the body in space, and \(\left(x, y, z\right)\) represents, typically, the position of some point in the rigid body. Once we know these 6 numbers, we know (i.e. can compute) the location of every point in the rigid body.

One of many ways to describe rigid body kinematics in 3D space; all representations need 6 numbers.

These numbers are also referred to as degrees-of-freedom (DOFs).

A camera captures the spatial distribution of light arriving from the environment — it records the direction from which light arrives, not the distance it has travelled.

Camera optics produce an image of the world, captured by an image sensor (CCD or CMOS) in digital cameras, or by film in traditional cameras.

An image \(I\left(x, y\right)\) is a 2D distribution of brightness of light. For a camera, the 2D surface is a rectangular region \(\Omega\). \[ I: \Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^2 \mapsto \mathbb{R}_{+}; \quad \Omega \ni \left(x, y\right) \mapsto I\left(x, y\right) \in \mathbb{R}_{+} \]

Image intensity \(I\left(x, y\right)\) at any location \(\left(x, y\right)\) is determined by the amount of light entering the camera through its optical system that directs the light to the image sensor.

Geometry of the optics determines the mapping of the spatial location of a source of light in the world \(\left(x^W, y^W, z^W\right)\) to a spatial location of the image \(\left(x^I, y^I\right)\).

We will primarily focus on the geometry of the optics for the purpose of kinematics tracking using camera image.

Real cameras are not pinhole cameras. They employ lenses to collect more light for producing brighter images.

Camera-based movement tracking

Camera calibration — Zhang’s method

The method every calibration tool uses. It needs only a flat printed target — no machined 3D rig.

You provide

  • A checkered board of known square size.
  • Many photographs of it — 15 to 20 — held at different tilts, distances, and positions in the frame.

The software returns

  • The 5 intrinsic parameters.
  • The distortion parameters \(k_1, k_2, k_3, p_1, p_2\).
  • Where the board was in each photograph.

How it decides:

  • Positions of the corners on the checkered board are known.
  • Detects the corners for the given size; this provides the correspondence between the 3D points and the image points.
  • Searches for the set of numbers that best predicts where every corner actually appeared
  • Leftover mismatch, in pixels, is the reprojection error.

Camera-based movement tracking

Camera calibration — what to check

Calibration always returns numbers. So the question is never “did it run?” but “should I believe it?”

  • Were the boards tilted? Images with the board parallel to the camera carry the same information over and over — the fit becomes ill-conditioned.
  • Did the board reach the corners of the frame? Distortion is largest at the edges. Calibrate only from the middle of the image and the distortion parameters are guesswork.
  • Was the board actually flat? Paper on a clipboard curls. The method assumes a plane, and a bent target quietly biases every number.
  • Is the square size right? Get it wrong and the intrinsics are unaffected, but every distance you go on to measure is scaled by the same factor.
  • How large is the reprojection error? Typically well under half a pixel. But a low error on a bad set of images means the model fits those images — not that the camera is characterised.

A small residual is necessary, not sufficient. This is the same lesson as static calibration in Module 1 — the fit quality tells you about the fit, not about the instrument.

Single camera inverse problem

Can we estimate the position of points in 3D space from a single camera?

Let’s assume that we have calibrated the camera. \(\v{K}\) is estimated and \(\v{R} = \v{I}\) and \(\v{o} = \v{0}\) assuming camera coordinate is the world coordinate.

Forward problem: 3D point \(\v{r}_P^W\) in world to 2D point in the image \(\v{r}_p^S\). \[\lambda \v{r}_p^S = \v{K}_f \m{\v{I}_3 & \v{0}} \v{r}_P^W\]

\(\lambda > 0\) is an unknown here because we are operating with homogeneous coordinates, and \(\v{r}_p^S = \m{x_p^S \\ y_p^S \\ 1}\).

Inverse problem: Knowing \(\v{r}_p^S\), we would like to find the 3D points that would have produced it.

We can now invert the relationship to find the following,

\[ \v{r}_P^W = \lambda \v{K}_f^{-1} \v{r}_p^S \]

This is the equation of a ray from the camera’s origin, pointing in the direction \(\v{K}_f^{-1} \v{r}_p^S\) !

Practical issues

Our discussion, thus far, has entirely focused on the ideal scenario.

In practice there are several issues that require careful consideration.

  • Spatial sampling of the image plane: the image is recorded on a pixel grid, so a small or thresholded marker moves in pixel-sized steps. Each pixel corresponds to a larger distance in space the further away the marker is — roughly \(z/f\).
  • Image sensor noise: noise moves the detected centre from frame to frame, so a still marker appears to jitter.
  • Image distortion: removed by calibration — what is left is only as good as the calibration.
  • Identification of corresponding points: which blob is which marker, in every camera and every frame? Swapped labels corrupt the data without any warning.
  • Temporal synchronization between cameras: unsynchronized cameras see a moving point at different times, so the rays do not meet. The error grows with speed.

Pros/Cons with unconstrained inverse kinematics:

Pros

  • Transparent: every segment pose comes straight from its own markers.
  • Ease of implementation: simple and easy to implement with good support.
  • Assumes nothing about the joints: whatever the joint does shows up in the result.

Cons

  • Anatomical constraints are ignored: nothing imposes the joint structure or fixed segment lengths.
  • Soft-tissue artefact passes straight through: nothing constrains it.
  • Needs three markers per segment, always: lose one and that segment’s pose is gone for those frames.
  • Errors feed into subsequent calculations: errors in joint angles will impact inverse dynamics calculations.

Some of these issues are addressed by the constrained (model-based) inverse kinematics approach: fit a kinematic model with joint constraints to all markers at once, minimising marker error (Lu & O’Connor, 1999). It suppresses artefact and keeps the chain intact, at the price of imposing a joint model that may be incorrect for the given subject.

Constrained inverse kinematics

Measured markers (blue) and the model’s markers (orange).

Start from a model of the skeleton: rigid segments of fixed length, joined by joints with a chosen number of DOFs (hinge knee, ball-and-socket hip…). Its configuration is one vector of generalised coordinates \(\v{q}\) — the joint angles themselves.

Markers are placed on the model at the same anatomical spots as on the subject. For any \(\v{q}\), the model predicts where every marker should be: \(\hat{\v{r}}_{m_i}^{A}(\v{q})\).

At each instant, find the pose that puts the model’s markers closest to the measured ones, all at once: \[\v{q}^{*} = \arg\min_{\v{q}} \sum_{i} w_i \left\Vert \v{r}_{m_i}^{A} - \hat{\v{r}}_{m_i}^{A}(\v{q}) \right\Vert^2\] A weighted least-squares fit; the joint constraints are built into the model, so they can never be violated.

What it buys: joint angles come out directly; uncorrelated marker noise is averaged across all markers rather than passed through; a lost marker is tolerated; segments never come apart.

What it costs: the model must first be scaled to the subject; any joint the model gets wrong (a knee that is not a hinge, a lax joint) is forced to behave as the model says — and the residual marker error is the only warning you get; soft-tissue artefact moves markers together, so it is not averaged away — the constraints can only push it into the joint angles the model allows.

Why a single image is enough. The tag is a plane of known size with four corners at known positions in its own frame \(W\) — and, being flat, every point on it has \(z_W = 0\).

Put that into our camera model: \[\lambda\,\v{r}_p^{S} = \v{K}_f \m{\v{r}_1 & \v{r}_2 & \v{r}_3 & \v{o}_{W}^{C}} \m{x_W \\ y_W \\ 0 \\ 1} = \underbrace{\v{K}_f \m{\v{r}_1 & \v{r}_2 & \v{o}_{W}^{C}}}_{\v{H},\ 3\times3} \m{x_W \\ y_W \\ 1}\]

The third column of \(\v{R}_W^C\) multiplies \(z_W = 0\) and drops out. What is left, \(\v{H}\), is a \(3 \times 3\) map from tag coordinates to pixels — exactly the planar case we met in Zhang’s calibration, with the tag playing the part of the checkerboard.

Counting: each corner gives two equations (its \(x_S\) and \(y_S\)); four corners give eight. \(\v{H}\) has nine entries but is defined only up to scale, so eight unknowns. The four corners determine \(\v{H}\) — and the code has told us which corner is which.

Tracking with fiducial markers — from \(\v{H}\) to the pose

With the camera calibrated, \(\v{K}_f\) is known, and \(\v{H}\) gives back the pose of the tag in the camera: \[\v{K}_f^{-1}\v{H} = c\m{\v{r}_1 & \v{r}_2 & \v{o}_{W}^{C}}\]

  1. The scale \(c\): the columns of a rotation matrix have unit length, so \(c\) is read off \(\Vert\v{r}_1\Vert\) and \(\Vert\v{r}_2\Vert\).
  2. The metric: the tag’s printed side \(d\) turns tag units into millimetres — that is how a single camera knows how far away the tag is.
  3. The missing column: \(\v{r}_3 = \v{r}_1 \times \v{r}_2\), since the columns of a rotation are orthonormal.
  4. The sign: \(\pm c\) both fit; the tag must be in front of the camera, which picks one.
  5. Tidying up: noise means \(\m{\v{r}_1 & \v{r}_2 & \v{r}_3}\) is not quite a rotation; nudge it to the nearest one.

So one tag is a complete marker cluster: position and orientation of the segment it is stuck to, from one camera, with no triangulation and no correspondence problem — the tag tells you its own ID.

Inertial measurement units

What an IMU is

An inertial measurement unit is a chip carrying three accelerometers and three gyroscopes, one of each along every axis of the chip’s own frame \(S\). Most also carry a three-axis magnetometer.

It reports six numbers, typically a few hundred times a second:

  • \(\v{a}_S^S(t)\): what the accelerometers feel, in the sensor frame.
  • \(\boldsymbol{\omega}_S^S(t)\): the chip’s angular velocity, in the sensor frame.

No reference outside the chip. A camera measures a marker against the camera; an encoder measures a shaft against its housing. An IMU measures its own motion against inertial space. That is its whole appeal, and the source of every one of its limitations.

Built as MEMS: micro-machined silicon, a few millimetres across, costing a few hundred rupees. In every phone, watch, drone, and most wearable movement sensors.

We take the two sensors in turn, then ask what the pair can and cannot tell us about a limb.

Inertial measurement units

Accelerometer — the mechanism

Newton on the mass, with the spring and damper pulling it towards the case: \[M \ddot{x}_M = K\,(x_S - x_M) + B\,(\dot{x}_S - \dot{x}_M)\]

Written for the gap \(y = x_S - x_M\), the thing the plates measure, with \(a_S = \ddot{x}_S\) the acceleration of the case: \[M\ddot{y} + B\dot{y} + K y = M\,a_S \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{Y(s)}{A_S(s)} = \frac{M}{Ms^2 + Bs + K}\] The gap is a second-order, low-pass response to the case’s acceleration. That is the entire accelerometer.

Inertial measurement units

Accelerometer — what the model says

Two numbers fall out of \(Y/A_S = M/(Ms^2 + Bs + K)\):

  • Static sensitivity \(= M/K\). Gap per unit of steady acceleration.
  • Natural frequency \(\omega_n = \sqrt{K/M}\). Above it, the mass cannot follow and the reading collapses.

They pull in opposite directions. A soft spring gives a large, easy-to-read gap and a low bandwidth; a stiff spring gives bandwidth and a gap too small to see.

MEMS parts choose stiffness: \(\omega_n\) in the kilohertz, so the sensor is flat over anything a limb does, and a gap of nanometres, which is why it is read as a capacitance difference and not as a position.

Module 1 again: this is the dynamic characteristic of the sensor. Everything above \(\omega_n\) is not measured, and near \(\omega_n\) it is exaggerated.

Inertial measurement units

Accelerometer — gravity is in the signal

The spring cannot tell the difference between the case accelerating upward and the case sitting still while gravity pulls the mass down. So the accelerometer reports, in its own frame, \[\v{a}_S^S(t) = \v{R}_E^S(t)\left(\v{a}_S^E(t) - \v{g}^E\right)\]

Two consequences, both easy to forget:

  1. At rest it does not read zero. It reads \(+1\,g\) pointing up, along whichever of its axes happens to be up.
  2. The reading depends on the orientation \(\v{R}_E^S\) of the chip, which the chip does not know.

The quantity \(\v{a} - \v{g}\) is called the specific force. An accelerometer is a specific-force sensor; “accelerometer” is a slight misnomer that causes real mistakes.

Inertial measurement units

Accelerometer — what does each one read?

Sensor 1: \(\v{R}_E^S = \v{I}\) \[\v{a}_S^S = -\v{g}^E = \m{0 \\ 9.81}\]

Sensor 2: \(y_S\) points down \[\v{a}_S^S = \m{0 \\ -9.81}\]

Sensor 3: turned by \(\theta\) \[\v{a}_S^S = 9.81\m{\sin\theta \\ \cos\theta}\]

Sensor 3 is the interesting one: the reading contains \(\theta\). Turn it around and a still accelerometer is a tilt sensor.

Inertial measurement units

Accelerometer — tilt from a still sensor

Invert the reading of Sensor 3. Two ways:

  • One axis: \(\theta = \arcsin(a_x / g)\). Sensitivity \(\partial a_x/\partial\theta = g\cos\theta\), which goes to zero as the axis approaches vertical. The same slope problem as the face-on tag: near \(90^\circ\) a small error in \(a_x\) is a large error in \(\theta\).
  • Both axes: \(\theta = \operatorname{atan2}(a_x, a_y)\). Sensitivity \(g\) everywhere, and \(g\) itself drops out of the ratio. Always do this.

This is how a phone knows which way is up, how a goniometer app measures a joint’s range of motion, and what the range-of-motion lab does: one accelerometer per segment, the subject holds still, read the angle. Holding still is the condition. The next slide is what happens when it is broken.

Inertial measurement units

Accelerometer — a moving sensor

\[\v{a}_S^S = \v{R}_E^S\left(\v{a}_S^E - \v{g}^E\right)\]

Three numbers measured. Unknown: the orientation \(\v{R}_E^S\) and the motion \(\v{a}_S^E\). One equation, two unknowns, and no algebra separates them.

A reading of \(\m{0 & 9.81 & 0}^\top\) is a chip sitting level, or a chip tilted \(90^\circ\) and accelerating sideways at \(1\,g\), or anything in between.

The usual escape is to assume the motion averages to zero over a second or so and low-pass filter the reading, keeping gravity and discarding movement. It works for slow postural tasks. It fails exactly when movement is of interest: during a reach, a step, a heel strike, the “tilt” from a filtered accelerometer swings by tens of degrees that never happened.

What the accelerometer cannot give, the gyroscope can: orientation that does not care whether the segment is moving. At a price, which comes next.

Inertial measurement units

Gyroscope — the mechanism

A mass is driven back and forth at a known velocity \(\v{v}\). If the chip turns at \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\), the mass feels the Coriolis force \(\v{f} = 2m\,\v{v}\times\boldsymbol{\omega}\), at right angles to both. Plates on either side read the deflection it causes, exactly as in the accelerometer.

Three such structures, one per axis, give \(\boldsymbol{\omega}_S^S(t) = \v{R}_E^S(t)\,\boldsymbol{\omega}_S^E(t)\): the angular velocity of the chip, in the chip’s own frame. Gravity does not enter. Motion does not confound it. What it measures is a rate, not an angle.

Inertial measurement units

Gyroscope — from rate to angle

To get an angle, integrate: for one axis, \[\theta(t) = \theta(0) + \int_0^t \omega(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau\]

The integral keeps everything, including the errors. A bias \(b\) in the rate becomes an angle error \(b\,t\) that grows without limit. Noise becomes a random walk, growing as \(\sqrt{t}\).

The plot: a gyro lying still on the bench, integrated. A residual bias of \(0.05^\circ\)/s, small by consumer standards, is \(3^\circ\) a minute. In an hour the angle is meaningless.

Two things follow. Calibrate the bias before every session, with the sensor still. And never trust an integrated gyro angle on its own for longer than the task needs.

Can an IMU measure position?

Position needs the acceleration integrated twice, after gravity has been subtracted using an orientation that itself came from integrating the gyro.

A bias \(b\) in the acceleration becomes a position error \(\tfrac{1}{2} b\,t^2\). The plot: a still accelerometer with \(10\) mg of residual bias, about \(0.1\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), is \(5\) cm out after one second and metres out after ten.

So, no. Not for more than a second or two, and not without something else to reset it: a camera, a foot known to be flat on the floor at each step, a known start and end point.

What an IMU is good at is orientation. Its position is a by-product nobody should use.

Putting the two together

The two sensors fail in opposite ways:

Accelerometer tilt Integrated gyro angle
Short term wrong whenever the segment moves right
Long term right, as soon as the segment is still drifts away
Reference gravity, absolute the starting angle, relative

Sensor fusion is any rule that takes the gyro for the fast part and the accelerometer for the slow part. The simplest one, the complementary filter, in one line per sample: \[\hat\theta_k = \alpha\left(\hat\theta_{k-1} + \omega_k\,\Delta t\right) + (1 - \alpha)\,\theta^{\text{acc}}_k, \qquad \alpha \approx 0.98\] Mostly the gyro, pulled gently back towards gravity. A Kalman filter is the same idea with the weights chosen from the noise levels instead of by hand.

Gravity fixes two of the three angles. Rotation about the vertical, the heading, leaves gravity unchanged and is invisible to the accelerometer. For that the chip’s magnetometer plays the part of gravity, with the Earth’s field as the reference. Indoors, near steel, motors and wiring, that reference bends by tens of degrees, and so does the heading.

A joint angle from two IMUs

Fuse each IMU to get its orientation in the earth frame, \(\v{R}_{S_1}^E\) on the thigh and \(\v{R}_{S_2}^E\) on the shank. The orientation of one chip relative to the other is \[\v{R}_{S_2}^{S_1} = \left(\v{R}_{S_1}^E\right)^\top \v{R}_{S_2}^E\] and the knee angle is read from it, exactly as it was read from the two marker-defined segment frames earlier in this module.

Two things this quietly assumes.

  • The chip’s axes are the segment’s axes. They are not: the chip is stuck on wherever the strap holds, at some unknown angle to the bone. This is the sensor-to-segment alignment problem, solved by a calibration pose or movement, and it is the largest error in IMU joint angles in practice.
  • Both headings share a reference. They do only if both magnetometers see the same, undisturbed field. Flexion and extension, about a horizontal axis, survive a bad heading; rotation and abduction do not.

And the strap is skin. Soft-tissue artefact applies to an IMU as it does to a marker, with the added twist that the IMU feels the strap’s own vibration as acceleration.

Calibration, and what to read on the datasheet

Calibration you do yourself

  • Gyro bias: leave the sensor still for ten seconds, average each axis, subtract. Before every session; it changes with temperature.
  • Accelerometer bias and scale: the six-position test. Lay the sensor on each of its six faces; each axis should read \(+g\), \(-g\) and four zeros. Fit offset and gain per axis.
  • Check, don’t assume: after calibrating, the magnitude \(\|\v{a}_S^S\|\) at rest should be \(g\) in every orientation. If it is not, the axes are not orthogonal or the scales are off.

Numbers to find on the datasheet

  • Range: \(\pm 2\,g\) or \(\pm 16\,g\); \(\pm 250\) or \(\pm 2000\ ^\circ\)/s. Heel strike can exceed \(\pm 2\,g\); the reading clips and the integral is wrong forever.
  • Noise density: \(\mu g/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}\) and \(^\circ/\mathrm{s}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}\). Multiply by \(\sqrt{\text{bandwidth}}\) for the noise you will see.
  • Zero-rate and zero-\(g\) offset, and their drift with temperature: the bias you will be subtracting, and how fast it moves.
  • Bandwidth and output rate: the sensor’s own low-pass, and the sampling theorem from Module 1.

Values for the lab’s IMU: to be filled in from its datasheet in the lab handout.

The lab does both calibrations on the kit’s IMU, then uses it for joint range of motion. Keep the calibrated numbers: the drift plots on the previous slides are what the uncalibrated sensor does.

Inertial measurement units

IMUs — in one place

What they measure

  • Specific force \(\v{a} - \v{g}\) and angular velocity, in the chip’s own frame, with no external reference.
  • A still accelerometer is a tilt sensor; use both axes.
  • A gyro is a rate sensor; its angle is an integral and drifts.
  • Fused, they give orientation: two angles against gravity, the third against the magnetic field.

What they do not

  • Position, beyond a second or two.
  • The segment’s frame: only the chip’s.
  • Heading, indoors, reliably.

Against cameras

  • No line of sight, no lab, no capture volume. Works in a clinic corridor, at home, for a day.
  • No position, so no segment lengths, no step length without a model, no absolute location.
  • Calibration is yours: bias before every session; sensor-to-segment alignment before every placement.

The measurement question to ask

Is it an orientation I need, about a horizontal axis, over minutes? Then an IMU is the right instrument. Is it a position, a heading, or an hour of unsupervised recording? Then it is not, on its own.
